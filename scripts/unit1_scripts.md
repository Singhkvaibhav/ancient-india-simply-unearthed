## Episode 1: The First Toolmakers

**Inscription tip:** Lower: chopper and hand-axe, Middle: flake. Upper: blade. · **Sources lit:** dug up · **Timeline ribbon:** (-2000000, -10000) · **Plaque:** Old name, new name: Old Stone Age → Palaeolithic · **Exhibit:** Hand-axe · **Label facts:** Quartzite, shaped on both faces; First found at Pallavaram, 1863; Lower Palaeolithic: from about 2 million years ago

| Segment | Visual | Narration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Dig | DIG: brush reveals the artefact | Brush away the soil. A heavy stone appears, chipped on both faces to a point. Someone once held this in the hand, more than a million years ago. It comes from India, long before farms, towns or writing. Who made it, and what did they eat? |
| How do we know? | SOURCES: evidence tags light up | How do we know? There is no writing at all in this age. The only evidence is what people left behind: stone tools, animal bones, and the layers of soil that hold them. Archaeologists dug these up, and dated the layers. |
| The Frieze | FRIEZE: carved panel pans scene by scene | In 1863, a geologist named Robert Bruce Foote picked up a stone tool at Pallavaram, near Madras. It was the first Palaeolithic tool found in India. The earliest period is the Lower Palaeolithic, which begins about two million years ago. People made hand-axes, cleavers and choppers from quartzite, a hard stone. Sites include Attirampakkam in Tamil Nadu and the Soan valley in Pakistan. These people lived by hunting and gathering, and moved with the seasons. They had no pottery and no farms. They sheltered in caves and rock shelters, such as those at Bhimbetka in Madhya Pradesh. In the Middle Palaeolithic, from about one hundred thousand years ago, tools grew smaller. People made flakes, scrapers and points. The Upper Palaeolithic begins about forty thousand years ago. Tools are now thin blades. Modern humans, called Homo sapiens, appear. All of this happened in the Ice Age, called the Pleistocene, when the climate swung between cold and warm. The next episode follows people who learnt to grow food. |
| The Plaque | PLAQUE | Old name, new name. Textbooks often say Old Stone Age. The scholarly name is Palaeolithic, from Greek words meaning old stone. It is the same period, with two names. |
| Inscription | INSCRIPTION: tip chiselled in | Carve this tip into memory. Lower: chopper and hand-axe. Middle: flake. Upper: blade. Three tool types, three ages. Remember that, and the periods are easy to place. |

## Episode 2: The First Farmers

**Inscription tip:** Mesolithic: tiny tools. Neolithic: polished axes, pots, farms. · **Sources lit:** dug up · **Timeline ribbon:** (-7000, -2500) · **Plaque:** Old name, new name: New Stone Age → Neolithic · **Exhibit:** Polished stone axe · **Label facts:** Ground and polished, not chipped; Neolithic farmers settled at Mehrgarh; Wheat, barley, pottery and villages appear

| Segment | Visual | Narration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Dig | DIG: brush reveals the artefact | The brush reveals a smooth, polished axe, ground on a stone until its edge was sharp. This is a Neolithic tool. People who made it were no longer only hunting. They were planting seeds, and keeping animals. What changed when people began to farm? |
| How do we know? | SOURCES: evidence tags light up | How do we know? Again, from the ground. Archaeologists dug up pits, post-holes, burnt grains and animal bones. At some sites even the burials show how people lived. There are still no written records. |
| The Frieze | FRIEZE: carved panel pans scene by scene | After the Palaeolithic comes the Mesolithic, from about ten thousand years ago. Tools became tiny stone blades called microliths. People still hunted and gathered, but they also herded animals. At Bhimbetka, rock paintings show hunters and dances. Then came the Neolithic, the New Stone Age. The best-known early site is Mehrgarh, in Baluchistan, dated to about seven thousand BCE. Here people grew wheat and barley, and lived in mud-brick houses. At Burzahom in Kashmir, people lived in pits dug into the ground. Dogs were buried with their masters. At Koldihwa in Uttar Pradesh, grains of rice have been found. In the south, at Maski, Brahmagiri and Piklihal in Karnataka, people kept cattle. They left behind ash mounds, which are heaps of burnt dung. Neolithic people ground and polished their stone axes. They also made pottery, and ground grain on stone querns. Farming meant a fixed home, stored food, and in time, villages. This is the foundation of everything that follows. |
| The Plaque | PLAQUE | Old name, new name. Many books say New Stone Age. The scholarly word is Neolithic, from Greek words meaning new stone. It is the age of polished tools, pottery and the first farms. |
| Inscription | INSCRIPTION: tip chiselled in | Carve this tip into memory. Mesolithic: tiny tools. Neolithic: polished axes, pots, farms. Tiny blades first, then polished axes and farms. Put them in that order in your answer. |
| Dig Quiz | QUIZ: three artefact cards, then Mains | Dig Quiz. Pause and name it. Number one. Name this smooth, ground tool of the Neolithic age. The answer: a polished stone axe. Number two. This tiny blade was set into a handle. What is such a tool called? The answer: a microlith. Number three. Which site in Baluchistan shows the earliest farming in South Asia? The answer: Mehrgarh. Now one line for Mains. Discuss the importance of the Neolithic revolution. |
| The Gallery | GALLERY: exhibit placed, label | The polished axe goes into its case, next to the hand-axe, for comparison. Three facts to take away. Ground and polished, not chipped. Neolithic farmers settled at Mehrgarh. Wheat, barley, pottery and villages appear. Next gallery: Copper and Painted Pots. |

## Episode 3: Copper and Painted Pots

**Inscription tip:** Chalco is copper. Painted pots, village homes, no plough. · **Sources lit:** dug up · **Timeline ribbon:** (-3000, -1000) · **Plaque:** Old name, new name: Copper Age → Chalcolithic · **Exhibit:** Painted pot · **Label facts:** Red ware with black painted designs; Chalcolithic: copper used with stone; Villages at Ahar, Malwa and Jorwe

| Segment | Visual | Narration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Dig | DIG: brush reveals the artefact | A painted pot comes out of the soil, red with black designs. Beside it lies a little copper. People of this time still used stone, but they had also learnt to work copper. Why did people start using metal? |
| How do we know? | SOURCES: evidence tags light up | How do we know? Once more, the evidence is dug up. Archaeologists found village houses, pots, copper tools and burials. We do not have their writing, so we learn about them from the remains. |
| The Frieze | FRIEZE: carved panel pans scene by scene | The word Chalcolithic means stone and copper. People used both. Most of these cultures were village cultures, spread over a wide area. In Rajasthan, the Ahar and Banas culture had copper tools, dated from about three thousand to fifteen hundred BCE. In Madhya Pradesh, the Malwa culture, at Navdatoli and Eran, made some of the finest painted pottery. In Maharashtra, the Jorwe culture, at Inamgaon, built houses of wattle and daub, which is woven sticks plastered with mud. Inamgaon has a granary and a large canal. People grew wheat, barley, rice, millet and lentils. They kept cattle, sheep, goats and pigs. They used the hoe, not the plough. The dead were buried, often inside the house, and children in pots. Away in the Ganga plains, hoards of copper tools have been found, known as the copper hoards. These small villages were not cities. But they show how metal and farming spread across India. |
| The Plaque | PLAQUE | Old name, new name. Older books say Copper Age. The usual term is Chalcolithic, from Greek words for copper and stone. It reminds us that people still used stone alongside copper. |
| Inscription | INSCRIPTION: tip chiselled in | Carve this tip into memory. Chalco is copper. Painted pots, village homes, no plough. Copper with stone, villages with pots, and a hoe in place of the plough. That is the Chalcolithic. |
| Dig Quiz | QUIZ: three artefact cards, then Mains | Dig Quiz. Pause and name it. Number one. This red pot with black paint comes from which age: Palaeolithic or Chalcolithic? The answer: Chalcolithic. Number two. Name the metal of these hoards found in the Ganga plains. The answer: copper. Number three. Chalcolithic farmers used a hoe. Did they use the plough? The answer: no. Now one line for Mains. Why is the Chalcolithic called a village culture? |

## Episode 5: Life in an Indus City

**Inscription tip:** Wheat, wheels, weights, writing: four W marks of Harappan trade. · **Sources lit:** dug up, inscriptions · **Timeline ribbon:** (-2600, -1900) · **Plaque:** Scholars disagree: A written language / A set of marks · **Exhibit:** Carnelian beads · **Label facts:** Carnelian, drilled and polished; Made at Lothal and Chanhudaro; Traded as far as Mesopotamia

| Segment | Visual | Narration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Dig | DIG: brush reveals the artefact | The brush reveals a string of beads, red as the setting sun. They are carnelian, a stone that does not come from the city where they were found. Somebody carried it, drilled it and sold it. Who traded these beads, and where did they go? |
| How do we know? | SOURCES: evidence tags light up | How do we know? Archaeologists dug up workshops, beads, copper tools and weights. Seals found abroad also help. Harappan seals have turned up in Mesopotamia, and Mesopotamian texts name a distant land, Meluhha, which many scholars link to the Indus region. |
| The Frieze | FRIEZE: carved panel pans scene by scene | Start with the fields. Harappan farmers grew wheat and barley, peas and sesame. They were among the first to grow cotton. Rice is known from Lothal. They kept cattle, buffalo, sheep and goats. Now the workshops. Potters used the wheel, and painted red pots with black designs. Craftsmen made bronze, which is copper mixed with tin. They made beads of carnelian, and seals of steatite. There is no iron. Trade was the heart of the cities. Goods moved by bullock cart, and by boat. Copper came from Rajasthan. Lapis lazuli came from Afghanistan. Seashells came from the coast. Goods also went overseas. Mesopotamian records speak of Dilmun, Magan and Meluhha. Dilmun is usually linked with Bahrain, and Magan with Oman. Meluhha is usually linked with the Indus region. Trade needs fairness. The Harappans used standard weights, cubes of chert stone, in neat ratios. And they wrote. The script has about four hundred signs, on seals and pots. The lines are short. Reading them is still an unsolved puzzle. |
| The Plaque | PLAQUE | Scholars disagree. The Indus script has not been read. One view says the signs record a language, and many guesses have been made about which one. Another view says the signs are too short to be a language, and may only mark names, goods or status. Neither view has been proved. |
| Inscription | INSCRIPTION: tip chiselled in | Carve this tip into memory. Wheat, wheels, weights, writing:. four W marks of Harappan trade. Fields, workshops, scales and signs. Four ideas that cover the economy of a Harappan city. |
| Dig Quiz | QUIZ: three artefact cards, then Mains | Dig Quiz. Pause and name it. Number one. Name this red stone bead, made in workshops at Lothal and Chanhudaro. The answer: carnelian. Number two. Mesopotamian texts call the Indus region by what name? The answer: Meluhha. Number three. Harappan pots are red. What colour were the painted designs? The answer: black. Now one line for Mains. Examine the trade links of the Harappan civilisation. |

## Episode 6: The Fading of the Indus

**Inscription tip:** Rivers, trade, no invaders: cities became villages. · **Sources lit:** dug up · **Timeline ribbon:** (-1900, -1300) · **Plaque:** Scholars disagree: Environment / Society and trade · **Exhibit:** Chert weights · **Label facts:** Cubes of chert, in neat ratios; Standard weights fade after 1900 BCE; No single cause is accepted

| Segment | Visual | Narration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Dig | DIG: brush reveals the artefact | The brush reveals a set of cubic stone weights. In the early layers they are neat and standard. In the later layers they are gone. The cities that once used them were fading. Why did the great cities fade away? |
| How do we know? | SOURCES: evidence tags light up | How do we know? We read the soil. Archaeologists dug up late layers at Harappa and other sites, and study old river beds and climate. The script is still unread, so no document tells us why. |
| The Frieze | FRIEZE: carved panel pans scene by scene | Around nineteen hundred BCE, the mature cities declined. This is called the Late Harappan phase. Town planning weakened. The drains were not kept up. Standard weights and the script go out of use. Long trade routes shrink. People did not vanish. Many moved east, toward the Ganga and Yamuna plains, and south into Gujarat and Maharashtra. Villages replaced cities. At Harappa, a later cemetery, called Cemetery H, shows new burial customs. Rivers matter in this story. The Ghaggar-Hakra river system seems to have dried up in parts. The Indus is also known to shift its course. In 1944, the archaeologist Mortimer Wheeler suggested that invaders destroyed the cities. Most scholars today find that evidence weak. The question is still open, and historians keep testing each idea against new finds. |
| The Plaque | PLAQUE | Scholars disagree. Why did the cities decline? One view stresses the environment: changing climate, drying rivers and floods. Another view stresses society: the end of long-distance trade and the breakdown of city life. Many scholars combine the two. |
| Inscription | INSCRIPTION: tip chiselled in | Carve this tip into memory. Rivers, trade, no invaders:. cities became villages. Remember rivers, trade and movement. Do not blame a single cause in your answer. |
| Dig Quiz | QUIZ: three artefact cards, then Mains | Dig Quiz. Pause and name it. Number one. Name these standard cubes that disappear in the Late Harappan phase. The answer: weights. Number two. Which later cemetery at Harappa shows new burial customs? The answer: Cemetery H. Number three. Which archaeologist suggested in 1944 that invaders destroyed the cities? The answer: Mortimer Wheeler. Now one line for Mains. Critically examine the causes of the decline of the Harappan civilisation. |
