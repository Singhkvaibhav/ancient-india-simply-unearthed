## Episode 34: Harsha and the Pilgrim

**Inscription tip:** 606 to 647, Kannauj capital, Hiuen Tsang watched. · **Sources lit:** texts, travellers' accounts, inscriptions · **Timeline ribbon:** (606, 647) · **Plaque:** Old name, new name: Hiuen Tsang → Xuanzang · **Exhibit:** Dharma wheel · **Label facts:** A wheel, the symbol of dharma; Harsha ruled from 606 to 647 CE; Hiuen Tsang visited his court

| The Dig | DIG: brush reveals the artefact | The brush clears a wheel carved with spokes, the wheel of dharma. It stands for a faith that a king, Harsha, supported with all his wealth. Every five years, he is said to have given away everything but his clothes. Why would a king give away everything every five years? |
| How do we know? | SOURCES: evidence tags light up | How do we know? The Harshacharita was written by Bana, Harsha's court poet. Hiuen Tsang, a Chinese monk, travelled in India for fourteen years. And inscriptions, such as the Banskhera plate and the Aihole inscription, give more. |
| The Frieze | FRIEZE: carved panel pans scene by scene | Harshavardhana was born into the Pushyabhuti family of Thanesar. His brother Rajyavardhana was killed by Shashanka of Gauda. Harsha became king in 606 CE, at sixteen. He made Kannauj his capital, and united much of north India. He then moved against the south, but was defeated by the Chalukya king Pulakeshin the Second on the Narmada, about 618 CE. Hiuen Tsang visited his court, and stayed at Nalanda. He describes a great assembly at Kannauj, and a gathering at Prayag, held every five years, where Harsha gave away his wealth. Harsha was also a writer. He is credited with three plays: Ratnavali, Priyadarshika and Nagananda. His state was less centralised than the Mauryas, and relied on feudatories. Officers were often paid with land. Harsha died in 647 CE without an heir, and his empire broke up. |
| The Plaque | PLAQUE | Old name, new name. The Chinese pilgrim is now written Xuanzang in modern romanisation. Older books spell him Hiuen Tsang, or Hsuan-tsang. He walked to India, studied at Nalanda, and returned with hundreds of Buddhist texts. |

| The Dig | DIG: brush reveals the artefact | The brush clears a stone boat, with a tall mast. It stands for the port of Mamallapuram, where Pallava kings carved temples out of single rocks, beside the sea. Why carve a temple out of one rock? |
| How do we know? | SOURCES: evidence tags light up | How do we know? Pallava copper plates and stone inscriptions give lists of kings. Archaeologists dug up the port at Mamallapuram. The Tamil hymns of the Nayanars and Alvars describe the age. |
| The Frieze | FRIEZE: carved panel pans scene by scene | The Pallavas ruled from Kanchipuram, about the sixth to the ninth century CE. Simhavishnu, about 575 CE, defeated the Kalabhras and began the great age. His son Mahendravarman the First was a patron of rock-cut temples, and wrote a comic play, the Mattavilasa Prahasana. Narasimhavarman the First, called Mamalla, defeated the Chalukya king Pulakeshin the Second, and took his capital, Vatapi, in 642 CE. He founded Mamallapuram. There, the Pancha Rathas are five temples carved from single rocks, with chariot-like shapes. The Shore Temple, built by Rajasimha, is a structural temple of stone blocks, beside the sea. At Kanchipuram, Rajasimha built the Kailasanatha temple. In these centuries, the bhakti poets, the Nayanars and Alvars, sang in Tamil. Their songs carried devotion to the people. |
| The Plaque | PLAQUE | Old name, new name. The Pallava port is called Mamallapuram, after Mamalla, the title of Narasimhavarman. The modern name of the town is Mahabalipuram, and both names are in use. |

**Inscription tip:** Vatapi, Pulakeshin II, Aihole, Pattadakal. · **Sources lit:** inscriptions, dug up · **Timeline ribbon:** (543, 757) · **Plaque:** Old name, new name: Vatapi → Badami · **Exhibit:** Chalukya elephant · **Label facts:** A carved elephant of the Deccan; Chalukyas of Badami, 543 to 757 CE; Builders at Aihole and Pattadakal

| Segment | Visual | Narration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Dig | DIG: brush reveals the artefact | The brush clears a carved elephant, large and steady. Elephants carried kings into battle, and carried honour into stone. The Chalukyas of Badami left many of them in their temples. What did an elephant stand for in a king's army? |
| How do we know? | SOURCES: evidence tags light up | How do we know? The Aihole inscription, composed by the poet Ravikirti in 634 CE, praises Pulakeshin the Second. Archaeologists dug up and recorded the temples at Aihole, Badami and Pattadakal. |
| The Frieze | FRIEZE: carved panel pans scene by scene | The Chalukyas of Badami rose in Karnataka. Pulakeshin the First, about 543 CE, made Vatapi, now Badami, his capital. Pulakeshin the Second was the greatest king, ruling from about 610 to 642 CE. He defeated Harsha on the Narmada, and added much of the Deccan. The Aihole inscription of 634 CE, by the poet Ravikirti, praises his victories. In 642 CE, the Pallava king Narasimhavarman defeated him and entered Vatapi. Vikramaditya the First restored Chalukya power. The dynasty lasted until about 757 CE, when it fell to the Rashtrakutas. The Chalukyas were great builders. At Aihole, there are the Durga and Ladkhan temples. At Badami, there are rock-cut cave temples. At Pattadakal, the Virupaksha and Sangameshvara temples show a blend of northern and southern styles. |
| The Plaque | PLAQUE | Old name, new name. The Chalukya capital was called Vatapi in inscriptions. Today the town is called Badami, in Karnataka. It lies in a valley of red sandstone hills, and is famed for its cave temples. |

**Inscription tip:** Rajaraja: Thanjavur temple; Rajendra: Ganga and sea. · **Sources lit:** inscriptions, dug up · **Timeline ribbon:** (850, 1279) · **Plaque:** Old name, new name: Tanjore → Thanjavur · **Exhibit:** Chola gopuram · **Label facts:** A tiered tower of the Chola temple; Brihadeeswara, about 1010 CE; Rajendra’s fleet sailed in 1025

| Segment | Visual | Narration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Dig | DIG: brush reveals the artefact | The brush clears the stepped tower of a southern temple, rising in tiers. It was built by the Cholas, who ruled the Kaveri delta, and later sent a fleet across the sea. Why would a king send ships across the sea? |
| How do we know? | SOURCES: evidence tags light up | How do we know? Chola inscriptions are carved on temple walls, and the Tiruvalangadu copper plates record Rajendra's victories. Archaeologists dug up Chola sites. Few other early dynasties left such rich records. |
| The Frieze | FRIEZE: carved panel pans scene by scene | The Imperial Cholas rose about 850 CE, when Vijayalaya took Thanjavur. His successors made it a base. Parantaka the First, about 907 to 955 CE, ruled much of the Tamil country. Rajaraja the First, from 985 to 1014 CE, was the first great emperor. He added the north of Sri Lanka and the Maldives, and built the Brihadeeswara temple at Thanjavur, completed about 1010 CE. His son Rajendra the First, 1014 to 1044 CE, marched to the Ganga and founded a new capital, Gangaikondacholapuram. In 1025 CE, Rajendra sent a naval expedition against Srivijaya, in south-east Asia. It was among the few times an Indian fleet projected power across the sea. Chola trade ties with China and south-east Asia were strong. After Kulottunga, the dynasty weakened and ended about 1279 CE under the Pandyas. |
| The Plaque | PLAQUE | Old name, new name. The Chola capital Thanjavur was written as Tanjore in colonial times. Today the official name is Thanjavur. The Brihadeeswara temple there is a World Heritage Site. |

## Episode 38: Chola Village and Temple

**Inscription tip:** Thirty wards, pot and palm leaf, a child draws the lot. · **Sources lit:** inscriptions, dug up · **Timeline ribbon:** (850, 1279) · **Plaque:** Scholars disagree: A strong centre / A segmentary state · **Exhibit:** Bronze lamp · **Label facts:** A lamp given to a Chola temple; Uttaramerur: elections by lot; Temples as centres of village life

| Segment | Visual | Narration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Dig | DIG: brush reveals the artefact | The brush clears a bronze lamp, with a bowl for oil. Chola temples were lit by lamps like this, given by villagers and kings. The same temples hold, on their walls, the rules for electing the village council. How did a village choose its leaders a thousand years ago? |
| How do we know? | SOURCES: evidence tags light up | How do we know? The Uttaramerur inscriptions, from the time of Parantaka the First, record the rules of the village council. Archaeologists dug up Chola villages. Few places show village rule so clearly. |
| The Frieze | FRIEZE: carved panel pans scene by scene | Chola villages ran their own affairs. The village assembly was called the ur, or the sabha in Brahmin villages. The Uttaramerur inscriptions record the rules for the sabha, about 920 CE. Each village was divided into thirty wards, and each ward chose one member. Candidates had to own land, have a home, be of a certain age, and know the Vedas. They could not have held office in the last three years, or have been guilty of crime. Names were written on palm leaves, put into a pot, and a child drew one out. This was called the kudavolai system, or election by lot. The elected members formed committees, called variyams, for tanks, gardens and justice. The temple was the hub of the village. It owned land, employed many, lent money, and ran schools. The state took a land tax and measured fields carefully. |
| The Plaque | PLAQUE | Scholars disagree. How centralised was the Chola state? One view sees a strong central kingdom, with officers, land surveys and a large army. Another view sees a segmentary state, where local assemblies and temples held much of the real power, and the king's rule was mostly ritual authority. Both views use the same inscriptions. |
