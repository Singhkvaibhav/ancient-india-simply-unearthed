| The Frieze | FRIEZE: carved panel pans scene by scene | The first known Gupta ruler was Sri Gupta, followed by Ghatotkacha. The dynasty became great with Chandragupta the First, about 319 to 320 CE, when the Gupta era begins. He married Kumaradevi, a princess of the Licchavi clan. This brought him prestige and power in the Ganga plains. His son, Samudragupta, ruled from about 335 to 375 CE. The Allahabad pillar inscription, written by Harisena, lists his conquests. In the north, he defeated several kings and took their lands. In the south, he marched across the east coast, defeated twelve kings, and then restored them to their thrones as vassals. He performed the Ashvamedha sacrifice, and issued coins to mark it. Some of his coins show him playing the veena, which tells us he loved music. The historian V. A. Smith called him the Napoleon of India. Many scholars today find that label too simple. |

## Episode 29: Chandragupta II and the Golden Age

**Inscription tip:** Vikramaditya: Malwa won, Fa-Hien watched. · **Sources lit:** coins, travellers' accounts, inscriptions · **Timeline ribbon:** (375, 415) · **Plaque:** Old name, new name: Fa-Hien → Faxian · **Exhibit:** Gupta gold coin · **Label facts:** Gupta gold dinara; Of Chandragupta II, about 375 to 415 CE; Fa-Hien visited in his reign

| The Dig | DIG: brush reveals the artefact | The brush clears a gold coin, stamped with a king in armour. It is a dinara of the Gupta age. Gold coins like this were issued in large numbers. The age of Chandragupta the Second is often called a golden age. How can iron stay bright for sixteen hundred years? |
| How do we know? | SOURCES: evidence tags light up | How do we know? Gold dinaras of many kinds survive. Fa-Hien, a Chinese Buddhist monk, travelled in India for several years. The iron pillar at Mehrauli has an inscription naming a king Chandra. |
| The Frieze | FRIEZE: carved panel pans scene by scene | Chandragupta the Second ruled about 375 to 415 CE. He took the title Vikramaditya, the sun of valour. He defeated the Shaka rulers of western India, and gained Malwa and Gujarat, with its ports. Ujjain became a second capital. He made marriage alliances too. His daughter Prabhavati Gupta married a Vakataka king, and ruled as regent after his death. Fa-Hien visited about 399 to 414 CE. He found the people prosperous and the roads safe, and he noted that punishments were mild. Tradition says that nine scholars, the Navaratnas, adorned his court. Kalidasa is the most famous name. Scholars debate this tradition. In Delhi, the Mehrauli iron pillar, which has not rusted, is linked to a king named Chandra, often identified with him. |
| The Plaque | PLAQUE | Old name, new name. The Chinese monk, once written as Fa-Hien, is now usually spelled Faxian in modern Chinese romanisation. The name means Splendour of the Law. He left a record called A Record of Buddhist Kingdoms. |

## Episode 30: Gupta Society and Economy

**Inscription tip:** Samantas, agraharas, shrenis, Smritis, Eran 510. · **Sources lit:** inscriptions, texts, coins · **Timeline ribbon:** (320, 550) · **Plaque:** Scholars disagree: A golden age / A more mixed picture · **Exhibit:** Gupta weights · **Label facts:** Scales used for weighing gold; Shrenis organised trade and craft; Land grants shaped society

| Segment | Visual | Narration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Dig | DIG: brush reveals the artefact | The brush clears a set of balance scales, the kind a trader used for gold. Under the Guptas, trade in gold and cloth was brisk. But the golden age was not golden for everyone. Did everyone share in the golden age? |
| How do we know? | SOURCES: evidence tags light up | How do we know? Inscriptions record land grants and local events. The Smritis, law books such as Narada's, set out the rules of society. Gupta coins show how rich the state was, and how the gold content changed. |
| The Frieze | FRIEZE: carved panel pans scene by scene | The Guptas ruled through a mix of direct and indirect control. Many local rulers, called samantas, paid tribute and kept their lands. The empire was divided into provinces called bhuktis, and districts called vishayas. Land grants became common. Kings gave villages, tax-free, to Brahmins and temples, in grants called agraharas. This gave grantees rights over the land and its people. Trade in the Gupta age was strong at first. Guilds, called shrenis, organised crafts. But after about the fifth century, the gold content of coins fell, and some scholars see this as a sign of change in trade. The Smritis tightened the rules of society. Untouchables, called chandalas, lived outside the towns, as Fa-Hien noted. The earliest clear record of sati comes from an inscription at Eran in 510 CE. Women's property rights were limited. |
| The Plaque | PLAQUE | Scholars disagree. Was the Gupta age a golden age? One view says yes, because of peace, art, science and trade. Another view says the label hides inequality, falling trade late in the age, and the harder position of women and low castes. Historians now mostly say it was an age of achievement and of change. |

What were the merchant guilds called? The answer: shrenis. Number three. Where is the earliest record of sati, dated 510 CE? The answer: Eran. Now one line for Mains. Examine the social and economic changes of the Gupta period. |

## Episode 31: Science and Letters

**Inscription tip:** Aryabhata: zero, pi, Earth spins; Kalidasa: Shakuntalam. · **Sources lit:** texts, inscriptions · **Timeline ribbon:** (400, 550) · **Plaque:** Old name, new name: Arabic numerals → Hindu-Arabic numerals · **Exhibit:** Armillary sphere · **Label facts:** A ring model of the heavens; Aryabhata wrote in 499 CE; Zero and place value in use

| Segment | Visual | Narration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Dig | DIG: brush reveals the artefact | The brush clears a ring of metal, an armillary sphere, a model of the heavens. Astronomers of the Gupta age used instruments like this. One of them, Aryabhata, wrote that the Earth turns on its axis. Who first said that the Earth turns? |
| How do we know? | SOURCES: evidence tags light up | How do we know? From the texts themselves. The Aryabhatiya was composed in 499 CE. Plays and poems of Kalidasa survive. Inscriptions record the patronage of Gupta kings. |
| The Frieze | FRIEZE: carved panel pans scene by scene | Aryabhata, born in 476 CE, wrote the Aryabhatiya in 499 CE. He used place value and gave a good value of pi, about three point one four one six. He explained eclipses as shadows, not as demons, and he wrote that the Earth rotates on its axis. Varahamihira wrote the Panchasiddhantika and the Brihat Samhita, on astronomy, weather and many other topics. The decimal system, with a symbol for zero, was in use. Arab scholars later carried it west, and it became known as the Hindu-Arabic numerals. In medicine, the Charaka Samhita and Sushruta Samhita were in use. Vagbhata compiled later work. The age was also great in letters. Kalidasa wrote the play Abhijnanashakuntalam, and poems like the Meghaduta and Raghuvamsha. The Panchatantra, a book of fables, and the Amarakosha, a word list, also belong here. |
| The Plaque | PLAQUE | Old name, new name. The numerals we use are often called Arabic numerals. A better name is Hindu-Arabic numerals, because the system began in India, and Arab scholars carried it west. |
| Inscription | INSCRIPTION: tip chiselled in | Carve this tip into memory. Aryabhata: zero, pi, Earth spins;. Kalidasa: Shakuntalam. One scientist, one poet. Each with two works you can name. |

## Episode 32: Gupta Temples and Art

**Inscription tip:** Flat, brick, stone, tower: Sanchi, Bhitargaon, Deogarh. · **Sources lit:** dug up, inscriptions · **Timeline ribbon:** (400, 550) · **Plaque:** Old name, new name: Garbhagriha → Sanctum · **Exhibit:** Early temple · **Label facts:** A model of an early Gupta temple; Sanctum and a pillared porch; The shikhara first appears in stone

| Segment | Visual | Narration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Dig | DIG: brush reveals the artefact | The brush clears a small stone shrine with a tall top. Before the Gupta age there were no free-standing stone temples in India. Then they appeared, first simple and flat, then taller. When did a temple first get a tower? |
| How do we know? | SOURCES: evidence tags light up | How do we know? The temples themselves survive. Archaeologists dug up shrines at Sanchi, Tigawa, Bhitargaon and Deogarh. Inscriptions name donors and kings who paid for them. |
| The Frieze | FRIEZE: carved panel pans scene by scene | Early Gupta temples are small. The Temple at Sanchi, Temple Seventeen, has a flat roof and a square sanctum, called the garbhagriha. A small pillared porch is in front. At Tigawa and Bhitargaon, later temples grew higher. The Bhitargaon temple is built of brick and has a high tower, and carved terracotta panels. The Deogarh temple, in Uttar Pradesh, has the earliest surviving shikhara, or tower, in stone. In the Deccan, the caves of Ajanta were carved and painted. Ajanta paintings show scenes from the Jataka tales, stories of the Buddha's earlier lives. Metalwork was also fine. The Delhi iron pillar and a copper Buddha image from Sultanganj, over two metres tall, show metal skill. Gupta sculpture is known for its calm and balance, as in the Sarnath images. |
| The Plaque | PLAQUE | Old name, new name. The inner shrine of a temple is called the garbhagriha, which means the womb chamber. In English it is called the sanctum. It is the heart of every Hindu temple. |

## Episode 33: The Guptas Fade

**Inscription tip:** Skanda held the Hunas; Yashodharman broke Mihirakula. · **Sources lit:** inscriptions, coins · **Timeline ribbon:** (467, 550) · **Plaque:** Scholars disagree: Outside pressure / Inner weakness · **Exhibit:** Gupta sword · **Label facts:** A long sword of the late Gupta age; Skandagupta held back the Hunas; The empire broke into regions by 550 CE

| Segment | Visual | Narration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Dig | DIG: brush reveals the artefact | The brush clears a long straight sword, worn at the edges. It belongs to the last great age of the Guptas. Fresh invaders pressed from the north-west. Governors at home grew stronger than their king. Why do great empires fade? |
| How do we know? | SOURCES: evidence tags light up | How do we know? The Bhitari pillar inscription records Skandagupta's battles. The Mandasor and Gwalior inscriptions record the Huna kings and the one who defeated them. And Gupta coins contain less gold in later reigns. |
| The Frieze | FRIEZE: carved panel pans scene by scene | Kumaragupta ruled for about forty years, and kept the empire. After him came Skandagupta, about 455 to 467 CE. The Bhitari inscription says he defeated the Pushyamitras, and then the Hunas, a Central Asian people. He also repaired the Sudarshana lake, as an inscription at Junagadh records. After Skandagupta, the empire weakened. The Hunas came back under Toramana and his son Mihirakula, who ruled from Sakala, now Sialkot. About 530 CE, the Malwa ruler Yashodharman defeated Mihirakula, according to the Mandasor inscription. Meanwhile, governors and feudatories, such as the Maukharis and the Later Guptas, grew independent. The gold content of Gupta coins fell, and the state's income shrank. By about 550 CE, the empire had broken into regions. |
| The Plaque | PLAQUE | Scholars disagree. Why did the Gupta empire decline? One view points to outside pressure, especially the Huna invasions. Another view points to inner weakness: land grants that shrank the revenue, governors that grew independent, and falling trade. Most historians combine the two. |
| Inscription | INSCRIPTION: tip chiselled in | Carve this tip into memory. Skanda held the Hunas;. Yashodharman broke Mihirakula. Two kings, two blows against the Hunas, and an empire that still fell. |
| Dig Quiz | QUIZ: three artefact cards, then Mains | Dig Quiz. Pause and name it. Number one. Which Gupta king fought the Hunas? The answer: Skandagupta. Number two. Which Malwa king defeated Mihirakula about 530 CE? The answer: Yashodharman. Number three. What fell in Gupta coins in later reigns? The answer: their gold content. Now one line for Mains. Discuss the causes of the decline of the Gupta empire. |
