About 305 BCE, he faced Seleucus Nicator, the Greek ruler in the east. They made peace. Seleucus gave up lands in the west, in Afghanistan and Baluchistan. In return, Chandragupta gave him five hundred elephants. Seleucus sent an envoy, Megasthenes, to Pataliputra. He wrote that the city was guarded by a wooden wall with five hundred and seventy towers and sixty-four gates. Chandragupta ruled until about 297 BCE. Jain tradition says that he gave up his throne, and went south to Shravanabelagola. His son Bindusara carried on the work. By then the empire stretched from the Himalayas deep into the south. |

## Episode 17: Inside the Mauryan State

**Inscription tip:** Seven limbs, six committees, one-sixth share. · **Sources lit:** texts, travellers' accounts, inscriptions · **Timeline ribbon:** (-321, -185) · **Plaque:** Scholars disagree: Strongly centralised / Strong core, loose edges · **Exhibit:** Revenue barge · **Label facts:** A river barge for tax grain; Seven limbs of the state; Bhaga: a share of the harvest

| Segment | Visual | Narration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Dig | DIG: brush reveals the artefact | The brush clears a river barge, loaded with grain. In the Mauryan empire, rivers carried tax grain and tribute to the capital. An empire this big needed officers, spies and records. How do you run an empire without roads or phones? |
| How do we know? | SOURCES: evidence tags light up | How do we know? The Arthashastra describes an ideal state. Megasthenes describes what he saw at the capital. And Ashoka's edicts name officers. Each source has limits, so historians compare them. |
| The Frieze | FRIEZE: carved panel pans scene by scene | The Arthashastra describes the state as having seven limbs: the king, the ministers, the territory, the fort, the treasury, the army and the ally. The king stood at the top, advised by a council of ministers. Under them were officers called adhyakshas, who ran departments, such as trade, mines and weights. The empire was split into provinces, ruled by princes or governors. Taxila, Ujjain, Tosali and Suvarnagiri were the main centres. Below them were districts and villages. Spies, called gudhapurushas, kept the king informed. Megasthenes says the city of Pataliputra was run by six committees of five members each. The chief tax on farmers was the bhaga, a share of the harvest, often given as one sixth. The state also owned mines, forests and some farms. The army was large, with infantry, cavalry, chariots and elephants. Greek writers give very large numbers, which may be exaggerated. |
| The Plaque | PLAQUE | Scholars disagree. How tightly did the Mauryas rule? One view says the state was highly centralised, with many officers and strong control, as the Arthashastra and Megasthenes suggest. Another view says control was strong at the centre and looser at the edges, resting on local chiefs and distance. |
| Inscription | INSCRIPTION: tip chiselled in | Carve this tip into memory. Seven limbs, six committees. one-sixth share. Seven, six and one-sixth. Three numbers that anchor the Mauryan state. |

## Episode 18: Ashoka and the Dhamma

**Inscription tip:** Kalinga, regret, dhamma, welfare, missions. · **Sources lit:** inscriptions, texts · **Timeline ribbon:** (-268, -232) · **Plaque:** Old name, new name: Devanampiya Piyadasi → Ashoka · **Exhibit:** Rampurva bull capital · **Label facts:** A bull on a lotus capital; From a pillar at Rampurva; Raised by Ashoka, about 250 BCE

| Segment | Visual | Narration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Dig | DIG: brush reveals the artefact | The brush clears a bull, standing on a lotus-shaped capital. It once crowned a pillar at Rampurva. It was raised by Ashoka, the Mauryan emperor. Early in his reign he was a conqueror. Later he gave up war. Why would a king give up war? |
| How do we know? | SOURCES: evidence tags light up | How do we know? From his own edicts, carved on rocks and pillars. Buddhist texts, including the Sri Lankan chronicles, add stories. The edicts are the more reliable source, since Ashoka spoke in his own voice. |
| The Frieze | FRIEZE: carved panel pans scene by scene | Ashoka was the grandson of Chandragupta and the son of Bindusara. He ruled about 268 to 232 BCE. About 261 BCE, he conquered Kalinga, on the east coast. His thirteenth rock edict says that a hundred thousand people were killed, and a hundred and fifty thousand deported. He expressed deep regret. After Kalinga, he turned toward dhamma. This was not a new religion. It was a code of conduct: respect for elders, kindness to servants, tolerance of all sects, and non-violence. He appointed officers called dhamma mahamattas, to spread this policy. He also made journeys, called dhamma yatras. He cared for his people. He built roads, rest houses and wells, planted trees, and arranged care for the sick, and animals. He also sent missions to neighbouring lands. His son Mahendra and daughter Sanghamitra took Buddhism to Sri Lanka. |
| The Plaque | PLAQUE | Old name, new name. Ashoka's own edicts do not usually use his name. They call him Devanampiya Piyadasi, which means beloved of the gods, of gentle looks. Only a few minor edicts, such as the one at Maski, name him Ashoka. |
| Inscription | INSCRIPTION: tip chiselled in | Carve this tip into memory. Kalinga, regret, dhamma. welfare, missions. Five words give the arc of Ashoka's reign, from war to welfare. |

## Episode 19: Words in Stone

**Inscription tip:** Brahmi left, Kharosthi right, Prinsep read it in 1837. · **Sources lit:** inscriptions · **Timeline ribbon:** (-268, -232) · **Plaque:** Old name, new name: Dhamma (Prakrit) → Dharma (Sanskrit) · **Exhibit:** Edict tablet · **Label facts:** Brahmi, Kharosthi, Greek and Aramaic; Major and minor, rock and pillar; Brahmi read by Prinsep in 1837

| Segment | Visual | Narration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Dig | DIG: brush reveals the artefact | The brush clears a smooth stone, covered with small, neat letters. For centuries no one could read them. Then, in 1837, a scholar solved the puzzle. These are the edicts of Ashoka. Who first read these letters in modern times? |
| How do we know? | SOURCES: evidence tags light up | How do we know? Inscriptions are the key evidence. They are carved on rocks and pillars, and they are the oldest deciphered royal writing in India. Nothing needs to be dug up, because the words are in the open. |
| The Frieze | FRIEZE: carved panel pans scene by scene | Ashoka's inscriptions are found from Afghanistan to Karnataka. They were written in Prakrit. Most used the Brahmi script, read left to right. In the north-west, at Shahbazgarhi and Mansehra, the script is Kharosthi, read right to left. In Afghanistan, edicts have been found in Greek and Aramaic. There are several types. The Major Rock Edicts are fourteen long texts. The Minor Rock Edicts are shorter. The Pillar Edicts are seven texts on tall pillars, such as those at Delhi-Topra and Allahabad. Some edicts are cut in caves, such as at the Barabar hills. A few are personal. The Queen's Edict names Karuvaki, mother of Tivara. In 1837, James Prinsep, an officer of the East India Company, read the Brahmi script. The key was a Delhi pillar. The edicts speak in a clear voice: this is what I did, and this is what I ask my people to do. |
| The Plaque | PLAQUE | Old name, new name. The word is dhamma in Ashoka's Prakrit, and dharma in Sanskrit. Both words mean the right way to live. Ashoka's edicts always use the Prakrit form. |
| Inscription | INSCRIPTION: tip chiselled in | Carve this tip into memory. Brahmi left, Kharosthi right. Prinsep read it in 1837. Two scripts, one decipherer and a year. Those are the facts to remember. |
| Dig Quiz | QUIZ: three artefact cards, then Mains | Dig Quiz. Pause and name it. Number one. Which script is read from left to right in most edicts? The answer: Brahmi. Number two. ## Episode 20: Mauryan Art

**Inscription tip:** Chunar stone, mirror polish, animal capital. · **Sources lit:** dug up, inscriptions · **Timeline ribbon:** (-321, -185) · **Plaque:** Scholars disagree: Persian influence / A native tradition · **Exhibit:** Polished pillar · **Label facts:** One piece of Chunar sandstone; Mirror-like Mauryan polish; Topped by an animal capital

| Segment | Visual | Narration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Dig | DIG: brush reveals the artefact | The brush clears a tall column, and its surface gleams like glass. It is made of one piece of sandstone, polished until it shines. This shine is the signature of Mauryan stonework. How do you make stone shine? |
| How do we know? | SOURCES: evidence tags light up | How do we know? We have the monuments themselves. Archaeologists dug up the pillared hall at Kumrahar. Inscriptions on the pillars tell us who raised them, and why. |
| The Frieze | FRIEZE: carved panel pans scene by scene | Mauryan art has two streams. One is court art, made by royal order. The other is the art of the people. The great pillars are the best-known court art. Each is cut from a single piece of sandstone, quarried at Chunar, and polished to a high gloss. Each carries an animal capital, such as a bull, an elephant or a lion. The capital stands on an abacus, above a bell-shaped base. The Sarnath lion capital, which is our national emblem, is the best known. At Kumrahar, near Patna, archaeologists found the remains of a great pillared hall, thought to have had eighty pillars. At the Barabar hills, caves were cut into hard granite and polished inside. The Lomas Rishi cave has a carved front. The art of the people includes terracotta figures, and large stone figures called yakshas and yakshis, such as the polished yakshi from Didarganj. |
| The Plaque | PLAQUE | Scholars disagree. Where did Mauryan court art get its inspiration? One view points to Persian influence, such as the bell-shaped capital and the polish, because Persian craftsmen were at work in the north-west. Another view points to a native tradition, which had earlier worked in wood, and moved to stone. |
| Inscription | INSCRIPTION: tip chiselled in | Carve this tip into memory. Chunar stone, mirror polish. animal capital. Three things mark a Mauryan pillar: sandstone, polish and an animal on top. |
| Dig Quiz | QUIZ: three artefact cards, then Mains | Dig Quiz. Pause and name it. Number one. From which stone are Mauryan pillars carved? The answer: sandstone from Chunar. Number two. ## Episode 21: After the Mauryas

**Inscription tip:** Brihadratha killed, 185 BCE: Shungas, then Kanvas. · **Sources lit:** dug up, texts, inscriptions · **Timeline ribbon:** (-185, -28) · **Plaque:** Scholars disagree: A Brahmin reaction / Strain and weak heirs · **Exhibit:** Shunga chalice · **Label facts:** A flared vessel of Shunga times; Shungas ruled from about 185 BCE; Followed by the Kanvas

| Segment | Visual | Narration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Dig | DIG: brush reveals the artefact | The brush clears a vessel with a wide, flared rim. It is a chalice, from the age of the Shungas. After Ashoka, the empire did not last long. Who ruled after the Mauryas? |
| How do we know? | SOURCES: evidence tags light up | How do we know? Archaeologists dug up Shunga towns and the railings of Bharhut. The Puranas and a play, the Malavikagnimitra, name the kings. An inscription at Ayodhya mentions Pushyamitra's sacrifices. |
| The Frieze | FRIEZE: carved panel pans scene by scene | Ashoka died about 232 BCE. His successors were weaker, and the empire shrank. The last Mauryan king, Brihadratha, was killed about 185 BCE by his own commander, Pushyamitra Shunga, who took the throne. The Shungas ruled from Pataliputra and Vidisha. Pushyamitra performed the Ashvamedha sacrifice, according to an Ayodhya inscription. Patanjali, the grammarian, lived in his time. The Bharhut stupa was enlarged in Shunga times, with a carved railing. The Shungas gave way, about 73 BCE, to the Kanvas, a short-lived family of Brahmin kings. They ruled for about forty-five years. After them, the Magadhan kings were overtaken by new powers, in the Deccan and the north-west. |
| The Plaque | PLAQUE | Scholars disagree. Why did the Maurya empire fall? One view blames reaction among Brahmins to Ashoka's policies. Another view points to financial strain from the army and officers, weak successors, and distant provinces drifting away. Most historians now look to the second set of reasons. |
| Inscription | INSCRIPTION: tip chiselled in | Carve this tip into memory. Brihadratha killed, 185 BCE:. Shungas, then Kanvas. One killing, one date, two short dynasties. That is the aftermath of the Mauryas. |
