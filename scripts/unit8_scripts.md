## Episode 39: Three Powers, One Prize

| How do we know? | SOURCES: evidence tags light up | How do we know? Copper plates and temple inscriptions list the kings and their victories. An Arab merchant, Sulaiman, wrote in 851 CE about the Pratiharas, Palas and Rashtrakutas, and named them as great powers. |
| The Frieze | FRIEZE: carved panel pans scene by scene | After Harsha, Kannauj was the prize of north India. Whoever held it was seen as the ruler of the Ganga plains. This long contest is called the tripartite struggle, because three powers took part, from about 750 to 1000 CE. The Gurjara-Pratiharas, from the west, were led by Nagabhata the First and Mihira Bhoja, the greatest of them. The Palas, in Bengal and Bihar, began when Gopala was chosen king, about 750 CE. Dharmapala and Devapala were great kings. They supported Nalanda, and founded Vikramashila. The Rashtrakutas, in the Deccan, began with Dantidurga. Krishna the First built the great Kailasa temple at Ellora, cut from one rock. Amoghavarsha wrote of poetry and ruled for over sixty years. Each of the three took Kannauj at some point, but none held it long. The war weakened them all, and opened the way for new powers. |

## Episode 40: The Rajput Kingdoms

**Inscription tip:** Chauhan Ajmer, Paramara Dhar, Chandela Khajuraho. · **Sources lit:** inscriptions, texts, dug up · **Timeline ribbon:** (750, 1200) · **Plaque:** Scholars disagree: Older Kshatriya lines / Rise of new clans · **Exhibit:** Rajput fort · **Label facts:** A fort of the Rajput age; Chahamanas, Paramaras, Chandelas; Strong forts, strong clan ties

| Segment | Visual | Narration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Dig | DIG: brush reveals the artefact | The brush clears a stone fort, with crenellated walls and a single gate. From about the eighth century, a number of small kingdoms in northern and western India built forts like this. They are often called the Rajput kingdoms. Why did hill forts become the heart of a kingdom? |
| How do we know? | SOURCES: evidence tags light up | How do we know? Inscriptions and grants record the kings. Chronicles and poems praise them. The Rajatarangini, a history of Kashmir, was written by Kalhana. Forts and temples still stand. |
| The Frieze | FRIEZE: carved panel pans scene by scene | After the Pratiharas, north India split into many kingdoms. These are known as the Rajput kingdoms, though the term itself arose gradually. The Chahamanas, or Chauhans, ruled from Ajmer. Their best-known king was Prithviraj the Third. The Paramaras of Malwa had Dhar as their capital. Their ruler Bhoja was a patron of learning. The Chalukyas of Gujarat, also called Solankis, built the Dilwara temples at Mount Abu. The Chandelas of Bundelkhand built the temples of Khajuraho between about 950 and 1050 CE. The Gahadavalas held Kannauj. These kingdoms were often at war with each other, and they were often ruled by clan chiefs with strong ties of kinship. The forts of Chittor, Gwalior and Ranthambore remind us of this age. |
| The Plaque | PLAQUE | Scholars disagree. Where did the Rajputs come from? One view holds that they descended from older Kshatriya lines of the Ganga plains. Another view holds that some clans grew out of groups that entered India, such as the Hunas and Gurjaras, and took Kshatriya rank. A medieval tradition, the Agnikula legend, gives a third account, in which four clans arose from a sacrificial fire. |

## Episode 41: Arrivals from the West

**Inscription tip:** 711 Sind, 1025 Somnath, 1192 Tarain. · **Sources lit:** coins, texts, travellers' accounts · **Timeline ribbon:** (711, 1200) · **Plaque:** Scholars disagree: The chroniclers / Modern historians · **Exhibit:** Silver dirham · **Label facts:** Silver dirham with Arabic script; Arabs reached Sind in 711 to 712 CE; Al-Biruni wrote on India in about 1030 CE

| Segment | Visual | Narration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Dig | DIG: brush reveals the artefact | The brush clears a silver coin, with lines of Arabic script. It was struck far from India, yet it is found here. From the eighth century, people, goods and ideas came to the subcontinent from the west, by sea and by the passes. Why do coins from far away turn up in an Indian field? |
| How do we know? | SOURCES: evidence tags light up | How do we know? Arab and Ghaznavid coins survive. The Chachnama, a Persian text, tells the story of Sind. And Al-Biruni, a scholar, wrote a detailed book on India. Each source has its own point of view, so historians compare them. |
| The Frieze | FRIEZE: carved panel pans scene by scene | In 711 to 712 CE, an Arab army led by Muhammad bin Qasim entered Sind. Dahir, its ruler, was defeated. Arab rule stayed in Sind and Multan, and did not extend far. Arab merchants had long traded along the west coast, and settled in places such as Kerala and Gujarat. In the early eleventh century, Mahmud of Ghazni raided northern India many times, between 997 and 1030. The best-known raid was on Somnath, in 1025. His aim was wealth as well as power. With Mahmud came Al-Biruni, a scholar who learnt Sanskrit. His book, the Kitab-ul-Hind, describes Indian science, religion, society and customs, with fairness. In 1191, Prithviraj the Third defeated Muhammad of Ghor at Tarain. In 1192, Ghor won the second battle at Tarain. This opened the way for new rule in north India. Contact also meant new ideas, and the exchange of knowledge between India and the Arab world. |
| The Plaque | PLAQUE | Scholars disagree. How should we read these events? One view relies on the chroniclers of the time, who often described them in religious terms. Another view, held by many modern historians, stresses wealth, politics and trade as motives, and also the exchange of ideas. Most scholars treat the sources with care, since they were written by people on one side or the other. |

## Episode 42: Land, Lords and Villages

**Inscription tip:** Grant, samanta, local market: was it feudal? Two views. · **Sources lit:** inscriptions, texts, travellers' accounts · **Timeline ribbon:** (600, 1200) · **Plaque:** Scholars disagree: A feudal order / Not feudal · **Exhibit:** Land-grant plate · **Label facts:** A copper-plate land grant; Samantas and tax-free villages; Feudalism: a debate between historians

| Segment | Visual | Narration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Dig | DIG: brush reveals the artefact | The brush clears a copper plate, covered in lines of text and ending with a royal seal. It records a king's gift of a village. Thousands of such plates survive. Why did kings give away villages? |
| How do we know? | SOURCES: evidence tags light up | How do we know? Copper-plate grants survive in thousands. Smriti law books describe rights and duties. And travellers, such as Hiuen Tsang and Al-Biruni, describe villages and towns. |
| The Frieze | FRIEZE: carved panel pans scene by scene | From the Gupta age onward, kings gave land to Brahmins, temples and monks, and to officers. These were called land grants, and the grantees were often exempt from some taxes. Grantees often gained rights over the village, such as collecting revenue, and sometimes justice. A class of local lords, called samantas, grew up. They held land, kept soldiers, and gave loyalty to a king, in return for protection. Many towns declined in this period, and coins were fewer. Trade did not stop, but it often moved to local markets, and temple towns. Peasants grew crops on the land, and paid a share to the lord. Some were tied to the land. Historians use these facts to ask whether early medieval India was feudal. |
| The Plaque | PLAQUE | Scholars disagree. Was early medieval India feudal? One view, by R. S. Sharma, holds that land grants, local lords and falling trade created a feudal order, with peasants bound to the land. Another view holds that the picture is too simple. It argues that peasants stayed largely free, that trade and towns continued in many regions, and that European feudalism is not a good model. |
| Inscription | INSCRIPTION: tip chiselled in | Carve this tip into memory. Grant, samanta, local market:. was it feudal? Two views. Three terms and a question. Always give both sides in your answer. |

What were the local lords of this age called? The answer: samantas. Number three. Which historian argued for Indian feudalism? The answer: R. S. Sharma. Now one line for Mains. Was early medieval India feudal? Discuss both views. |

## Episode 43: Three Styles of Temple

**Inscription tip:** Nagara north: shikhara. Dravida south: vimana. Vesara mix. · **Sources lit:** dug up, inscriptions, texts · **Timeline ribbon:** (500, 1200) · **Plaque:** Old name, new name: Orissa → Odisha · **Exhibit:** Temple model · **Label facts:** A model of a temple tower; Nagara, Dravida and Vesara styles; Sanctum, hall and tower

| Segment | Visual | Narration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Dig | DIG: brush reveals the artefact | The brush clears a model of a temple, with a tower curved like a beehive. Temples in different parts of India have different tower shapes. A trained eye can tell the region from the roof. How can you tell a temple's region from its roof? |
| How do we know? | SOURCES: evidence tags light up | How do we know? The temples survive, and archaeologists dug up the sites around them. Inscriptions name donors and builders. The Shilpashastras are texts on the art of building. |
| The Frieze | FRIEZE: carved panel pans scene by scene | Indian temples have a common plan. The sanctum is the garbhagriha. In front is a hall, the mandapa. Above the sanctum rises a tower. Three styles are named after the shape of that tower. The Nagara style is found in the north. Its tower, the shikhara, curves inward like a beehive. The temples of Khajuraho, and the Sun Temple at Konark in Odisha, are Nagara. The Dravida style is found in the south. Its tower, the vimana, rises in stepped tiers, and gateways, called gopurams, grow taller in later centuries. The Pallava temples and the Brihadeeswara temple are Dravida. The Vesara style is a mix, found in the Deccan. It blends Nagara and Dravida features. The Hoysala temples at Belur and Halebidu, with star-shaped plans and very fine carving, are Vesara. Some scholars use these three terms for convenience, and point out that the old texts use them in various ways. |
| The Plaque | PLAQUE | Old name, new name. The Sun Temple at Konark is in the state once called Orissa. In 2011, the official English name became Odisha. The temple is a World Heritage Site, and a masterpiece of the Nagara style. |
| Inscription | INSCRIPTION: tip chiselled in | Carve this tip into memory. Nagara north: shikhara. Dravida south: vimana. Vesara mix. North, south and in between, and a tower for each. Name the tower and you know the style. |

| How do we know? | SOURCES: evidence tags light up | How do we know? Look at how the story was told. Tools, bones and pottery from the ground. Hymns, epics and poems. Inscriptions on stone and copper. Coins from many kings. Travellers' accounts from far lands. Each age needs a different kind of source. |
| The Frieze | FRIEZE: carved panel pans scene by scene | First came the toolmakers and the first farmers. Over two million years, people moved from chipped stone to polished axes, and from hunting to the first villages. Then came the Indus cities, about 2600 to 1900 BCE, with their grid streets, drains and seals that no one can yet read. Then came the Vedic age and the Mahajanapadas. Iron, ploughs and kingdoms grew up, along with thinkers like Mahavira and the Buddha. Then came the Mauryas, the first empire, and Ashoka's edicts in stone. After them came Greeks, Shakas, Kushanas, the Satavahanas and the Sangam south, with a rich trade by sea. Then came the Guptas, Harsha, the Pallavas, the Chalukyas, the Cholas and the Rajput kingdoms, until about 1200 CE. |
| The Plaque | PLAQUE | Old name, new name. Colonial writers, such as James Mill, divided Indian history into a Hindu period, a Muslim period and a British period. Modern historians prefer ancient, medieval and modern, because the old labels reduced the past to religion. |
