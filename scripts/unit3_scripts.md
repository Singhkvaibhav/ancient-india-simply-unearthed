## Episode 10: Sixteen Mahajanapadas

**Inscription tip:** Sixteen states, two republics, Magadha wins with iron and rivers. · **Sources lit:** dug up, texts, coins · **Timeline ribbon:** (-600, -400) · **Plaque:** Old name, new name: Pataliputra → Patna · **Exhibit:** Punch-marked coin · **Label facts:** Silver, stamped with small symbols; Used from about the sixth century BCE; A sign of growing trade and towns

| How do we know? | SOURCES: evidence tags light up | How do we know? Archaeologists dug up the fine black pottery and walled towns of the period. Buddhist and Jain texts list the sixteen states. Punch-marked coins show that trade was growing. |
| The Frieze | FRIEZE: carved panel pans scene by scene | After about six hundred BCE, the Ganga plains saw a second wave of cities. Iron tools, new farmland and trade made them possible. This is called the second urbanisation, after the Harappan cities. Early Buddhist and Jain texts list sixteen great states, the Mahajanapadas. They include Anga, Magadha, Kashi, Kosala, Vajji, Malla, Chedi, Vatsa, Kuru, Panchala, Matsya, Surasena, Assaka, Avanti, Gandhara and Kamboja. Most were kingdoms. But Vajji and Malla were ruled by assemblies, called gana-sanghas, a form of republic. Four states stood out: Magadha, Kosala, Vatsa and Avanti. In time Magadha won. Magadha was strong for good reasons. It had iron ore nearby, fertile land, and rivers for trade. Its forests gave it war elephants. Its early capital was Rajagriha, and later Pataliputra. New goods marked this age. Coins were struck, and a shiny black pottery, called Northern Black Polished Ware, spread across the plains. |

## Episode 11: The Jain Path

**Inscription tip:** Three jewels, five vows, twenty-four Tirthankaras. · **Sources lit:** texts, dug up · **Timeline ribbon:** (-600, -400) · **Plaque:** Old name, new name: Nirgrantha → Jain · **Exhibit:** Jain lamp · **Label facts:** A lamp, a symbol of knowledge; Three jewels and five vows; Teaching spread in Prakrit

| Segment | Visual | Narration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Dig | DIG: brush reveals the artefact | The brush clears a small clay lamp. Jain tradition remembers the lamps lit at the time of Mahavira's liberation, and the festival is still marked with lights. This faith did not rest on a creator god, but on conduct. Why would a lamp be central to it? |
| How do we know? | SOURCES: evidence tags light up | How do we know? Mostly from Jain texts, called Agamas, written in Prakrit. Archaeologists dug up Jain caves and inscriptions of later centuries. Our dates for Mahavira come from tradition, and they differ. |
| The Frieze | FRIEZE: carved panel pans scene by scene | Jain tradition speaks of twenty-four Tirthankaras, teachers who show the way across. The first was Rishabhanatha. The twenty-third was Parshvanatha. The twenty-fourth was Mahavira. Mahavira was born as Vardhamana, a prince of a clan near Vaishali in Bihar, about the sixth century BCE. Jain tradition gives 599 BCE, and many books give about 540 BCE. At thirty, he left his home. After years of hard discipline, he gained perfect knowledge. He was called Jina, the conqueror, and his followers were called Jains. His teaching rests on three jewels: right faith, right knowledge and right conduct. He taught five vows: do not harm any living being, which is ahimsa, speak truth, do not steal, do not own more than you need, and keep chastity. Jainism does not accept a creator god. It teaches karma and rebirth, and the path to liberation. Later, the community divided into the Digambara, the sky-clad, and the Shvetambara, the white-clad. Jain texts were written in Prakrit, so common people could follow. |
| The Plaque | PLAQUE | Old name, new name. Early Jain monks were called Nirgranthas, which means those free from bonds. Later, the followers of the Jinas came to be called Jains. |
| Inscription | INSCRIPTION: tip chiselled in | Carve this tip into memory. Three jewels, five vows. twenty-four Tirthankaras. Three, five and twenty-four. Say those numbers and the Jain outline follows. |

## Episode 12: The Buddha's Path

**Inscription tip:** Lumbini born, Bodh Gaya awake, Sarnath taught, Kushinagar rest. · **Sources lit:** texts, inscriptions · **Timeline ribbon:** (-563, -400) · **Plaque:** Old name, new name: Dhammachakka (Pali) → Dharmachakra (Sanskrit) · **Exhibit:** Footprints stone · **Label facts:** A footprint, a symbol of presence; Four Noble Truths, Eightfold Path; About 563 to 483 BCE

| Segment | Visual | Narration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Dig | DIG: brush reveals the artefact | The brush clears two carved footprints, with a wheel in the centre of each. Early Indian artists did not show the Buddha as a person. They showed his presence through symbols: footprints, an empty throne, a tree, a wheel. What do these footprints stand for? |
| How do we know? | SOURCES: evidence tags light up | How do we know? From Buddhist texts, the Tripitaka, written in Pali. Inscriptions help too. Ashoka's pillar at Lumbini marks the birthplace. The dates, about 563 to 483 BCE, are the most common textbook dates. |
| The Frieze | FRIEZE: carved panel pans scene by scene | Siddhartha Gautama was born about 563 BCE at Lumbini, in a Shakya clan of Kapilavastu. At about twenty-nine, he left home to seek the end of suffering. After years of search, he sat under a pipal tree at Bodh Gaya. There he gained enlightenment, and became the Buddha, the awakened one. His first sermon was given at Sarnath, near Varanasi, to five ascetics. It is called the turning of the wheel of dharma. He taught the Four Noble Truths. There is suffering. It has a cause, which is desire. It can end. And there is a path to its end. That path is the Eightfold Path, a Middle Way between pleasure and hard self-denial. It covers right view, thought, speech, action, livelihood, effort, mindfulness and concentration. He died at Kushinagar, about 483 BCE. Four places mark his life: Lumbini, Bodh Gaya, Sarnath and Kushinagar. Followers formed a sangha, an order of monks and nuns. |
| The Plaque | PLAQUE | Old name, new name. The wheel is called the Dhammachakka in Pali, the language of the early texts. In Sanskrit, it is the Dharmachakra. It is the same wheel, with two names. |
| Inscription | INSCRIPTION: tip chiselled in | Carve this tip into memory. Lumbini born, Bodh Gaya awake. Sarnath taught, Kushinagar rest. Four places, four steps: birth, awakening, first teaching and final rest. |
| Dig Quiz | QUIZ: three artefact cards, then Mains | Dig Quiz. Pause and name it. Number one. Where was the Buddha born? The answer: Lumbini. Number two. Where was the first sermon given? ## Episode 13: Councils and Stupas

**Inscription tip:** Rajagriha, Vaishali, Pataliputra, Kashmir: four councils. · **Sources lit:** texts, dug up, inscriptions · **Timeline ribbon:** (-483, -100) · **Plaque:** Old name, new name: Hinayana → Theravada · **Exhibit:** Stupa model · **Label facts:** A mound that holds a relic; Four councils, three baskets; Great stupas at Sanchi and Bharhut

| Segment | Visual | Narration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Dig | DIG: brush reveals the artefact | The brush clears a half-round mound of stone, topped by a small square railing and an umbrella. It is a stupa, a monument that holds a relic, a small piece of what remained after cremation. Why build a mound over a relic? |
| How do we know? | SOURCES: evidence tags light up | How do we know? Buddhist texts record the councils. Archaeologists dug up stupas at Sanchi, Bharhut and Amaravati. Inscriptions on the railings name the donors who paid for them. |
| The Frieze | FRIEZE: carved panel pans scene by scene | After the death of the Buddha, the teachings needed to be kept. Four councils met. The first, at Rajagriha, was about 483 BCE, under Ajatashatru. Upali recited the rules of the order, and Ananda recited the teachings. The second, at Vaishali, was about a century later, and ended with a split in the order. The third was at Pataliputra, under Ashoka, led by Moggaliputta Tissa. After it, missions were sent out, including to Sri Lanka. The fourth was in Kashmir, under Kanishka. Here the Mahayana branch took shape, with texts in Sanskrit. The teachings were collected in three baskets, the Tripitaka: Vinaya, Sutta and Abhidhamma. Stupas spread, with carved gateways and railings at Sanchi, Bharhut and Amaravati. Monks also lived in viharas, and prayed in chaitya halls. |
| The Plaque | PLAQUE | Old name, new name. The older branch of Buddhism is often called Hinayana, the lesser vehicle. That is a name used by its critics, and many now consider it unfair. The name Theravada, the way of the elders, is preferred today. |

## Episode 14: Magadha Rises

**Inscription tip:** Haryanka, Shishunaga, Nanda: three families, one rising state. · **Sources lit:** texts, dug up · **Timeline ribbon:** (-544, -321) · **Plaque:** Old name, new name: Rajagriha → Rajgir · **Exhibit:** Empty throne · **Label facts:** An empty throne, symbol of power; Haryanka, Shishunaga, Nanda; Overthrown about 321 BCE

| Segment | Visual | Narration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Dig | DIG: brush reveals the artefact | The brush clears a carved throne. No one sits on it. This empty seat stands for the kings who ruled Magadha, one dynasty after another. Each one added land, and each one added power. Who sat on the throne of Magadha? |
| How do we know? | SOURCES: evidence tags light up | How do we know? Buddhist and Jain texts and the Puranas list the kings. Archaeologists dug up the walls of Rajgir and the wooden posts of Pataliputra. The exact dates of early kings are uncertain. |
| The Frieze | FRIEZE: carved panel pans scene by scene | The first great dynasty of Magadha was the Haryanka. Bimbisara, about 544 BCE, grew his kingdom by war and marriage. He married princesses from Kosala, from the Licchavi clan and from Madra. He conquered Anga. His son Ajatashatru was bolder. He defeated Kosala and the Vajji republic. His army used a catapult and a covered war chariot. In his time, the first Buddhist council met. Udayin, his successor, founded Pataliputra at the meeting of the Ganga and Son. Next came the Shishunagas, who ended the power of Avanti. Then came the Nandas. Mahapadma Nanda is called ekarat, the sole ruler. The Nandas had great wealth and an enormous army, which Greek writers describe with awe. The last Nanda, Dhana Nanda, was overthrown about 321 BCE by Chandragupta Maurya. A new empire was about to begin. |
| The Plaque | PLAQUE | Old name, new name. The early capital of Magadha was Rajagriha, the royal house. Its ruins are at the modern town of Rajgir, in Bihar. The name has hardly changed, only shortened. |
| Inscription | INSCRIPTION: tip chiselled in | Carve this tip into memory. Haryanka, Shishunaga, Nanda:. three families, one rising state. Three dynasties in order, and Bimbisara and Ajatashatru for the first. That covers most questions. |

## Episode 15: Persians and Alexander

**Inscription tip:** Darius took Hindush. Alexander stopped at the Beas. · **Sources lit:** inscriptions, travellers' accounts · **Timeline ribbon:** (-518, -323) · **Plaque:** Old name, new name: Hindush / Sindhu → India · **Exhibit:** Greek helmet · **Label facts:** Crested bronze, Greek in style; Alexander crossed the Hindu Kush, 327 BCE; He fought Porus on the Jhelum, 326 BCE

| Segment | Visual | Narration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Dig | DIG: brush reveals the artefact | The brush clears a bronze helmet, crested and curved. It is Greek in style. Yet it is a find from the north-west of the subcontinent. Two great empires, Persian and Greek, reached this land before the Mauryas. What was a Greek helmet doing in India? |
| How do we know? | SOURCES: evidence tags light up | How do we know? Persian inscriptions at Behistun and Persepolis name a land called Hindush. Greek writers, such as Arrian, wrote about Alexander and what he saw. They were written long after, so they have to be read with care. |
| The Frieze | FRIEZE: carved panel pans scene by scene | First came the Persians. About 518 BCE, Darius the First, of the Achaemenid empire, took parts of the north-west, which his inscriptions call Hindush. It became a Persian province, a satrapy, and paid tribute in gold. One result was a new script. Kharosthi, written right to left, grew out of the Aramaic script used by Persian clerks. Then came Alexander of Macedon. He crossed the Hindu Kush in 327 BCE. The king of Taxila, Ambhi, joined him. In 326 BCE he fought King Porus on the river Jhelum, which the Greeks called the Hydaspes. The battle was hard, and Alexander honoured Porus and left him as his ally. At the river Beas, his tired soldiers refused to march further. Alexander turned back, and died in Babylon in 323 BCE. His conquest left no lasting empire. But it opened new routes, and Greek and Indian ideas began to meet. |
| The Plaque | PLAQUE | Old name, new name. The Persians called the land by the river, Hindush. The Greeks dropped the first sound and said Indos, and later India. All three names come from the great river Sindhu. |
| Inscription | INSCRIPTION: tip chiselled in | Carve this tip into memory. Darius took Hindush. Alexander stopped at the Beas. Persians first, Greeks second. Kharosthi from Persia, and a helmet from Greece. |
| Dig Quiz | QUIZ: three artefact cards, then Mains | Dig Quiz. Pause and name it. Number one. Which Persian king added Hindush to his empire? The answer: Darius the First. Number two. On which river did Alexander fight Porus? The answer: the Jhelum, or Hydaspes. Number three. Which script grew from the Aramaic of Persian clerks? The answer: Kharosthi. Now one line for Mains. Assess the impact of the Persian and Macedonian invasions on India. |
