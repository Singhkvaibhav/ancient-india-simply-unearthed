| The Frieze | FRIEZE: carved panel pans scene by scene | After the Mauryas, the north-west was open to foreign rulers. The first were the Indo-Greeks, also called the Bactrian Greeks. Demetrius was among the earliest, about 200 BCE. The most famous Indo-Greek king was Menander, called Milinda in Indian texts. The Milindapanha, a Buddhist text, records his questions to the monk Nagasena. Indo-Greek coins were fine and clear. They carry the ruler's portrait and a legend in Greek on one side, and in Kharosthi on the other. They were the first coins that can be firmly credited to named kings. Next came the Shakas, who came from Central Asia. Their best-known ruler was Rudradaman, about 150 CE. His Junagadh inscription is the first long inscription in classical Sanskrit. It records the repair of the Sudarshana lake. The Parthians, or Pahlavas, came after them. Gondophernes is their most famous king. The Shaka era, which starts in 78 CE, is still used in the Indian national calendar. |
| The Plaque | PLAQUE | Old name, new name. Indian texts called the Greeks Yavanas. The word is thought to come from Ionians, a Greek people. Today, the word Greek is used. Yavana was used in India for foreigners from the west, for a long time. |

## Episode 23: The Kushanas

**Inscription tip:** Kushana: gold, Kanishka, Peshawar, Silk Route. · **Sources lit:** coins, inscriptions, texts · **Timeline ribbon:** (30, 375) · **Plaque:** Scholars disagree: About 78 CE / About 127 CE or later · **Exhibit:** Kushana gold coin · **Label facts:** Gold, with Greek letters; Struck in the Kushana empire; Made when the Silk Route was rich

| Segment | Visual | Narration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Dig | DIG: brush reveals the artefact | The brush clears a gold coin, stamped with a king and figures from several lands: Greek, Iranian and Indian. The Kushanas ruled across the roads where those lands met. Why would a king put many gods on his coins? |
| How do we know? | SOURCES: evidence tags light up | How do we know? Kushana gold coins, in Greek letters, are everywhere. The Rabatak inscription in Afghanistan names Kanishka. And writers such as Ashvaghosha lived at his court. |
| The Frieze | FRIEZE: carved panel pans scene by scene | The Kushanas were a branch of the Yuezhi, a people from Central Asia. Kujula Kadphises united the tribes and took the north-west. His successor Vima Kadphises issued the first Kushana gold coins. The greatest Kushana king was Kanishka. He began his reign about 78 CE, and the Shaka era is traditionally linked to it, though some scholars give a later date. His capital was Purushapura, now Peshawar. He also ruled from Mathura. His empire stretched from Central Asia to the Ganga plains. Kanishka supported Buddhism. The fourth Buddhist council is said to have met in his time. He built a tall stupa at Peshawar. His court was home to Ashvaghosha, who wrote the Buddhacharita, and to the physician Charaka. The Kushanas controlled the Silk Route. Trade in silk, spices and gold passed through their lands, and made them rich. |
| The Plaque | PLAQUE | Scholars disagree. When did Kanishka rule? One view puts the start of his reign in 78 CE, which is why the Shaka era begins then. Another view places him later, in the early second century, and argues that the Shaka era was founded by someone else. The coins and inscriptions do not settle the question. |
| Inscription | INSCRIPTION: tip chiselled in | Carve this tip into memory. Kushana: gold, Kanishka, Peshawar. Silk Route. Four anchors for the Kushanas. Gold, a king, a capital and a road. |
| Dig Quiz | QUIZ: three artefact cards, then Mains | Dig Quiz. Pause and name it. Number one. Which Kushana king first issued gold coins? The answer: Vima Kadphises. Number two. Discuss the role of the Kushanas in trade and culture. |

## Episode 24: The Satavahanas

**Inscription tip:** Satavahana: mother-names, tax-free grants, torana gates. · **Sources lit:** inscriptions, coins, texts · **Timeline ribbon:** (-100, 220) · **Plaque:** Old name, new name: Andhras → Satavahanas · **Exhibit:** Torana gateway · **Label facts:** A torana, or stupa gateway; Funded in the Satavahana age; Kings used their mothers’ names

| Segment | Visual | Narration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Dig | DIG: brush reveals the artefact | The brush clears a stone gateway, carved with panels. It stands at a stupa in central India, but it was paid for by the Satavahanas, who ruled the Deccan. Their kings took names from their mothers. Why did a king take his mother's name? |
| How do we know? | SOURCES: evidence tags light up | How do we know? The Nashik inscription praises King Gautamiputra. Lead coins carry the names of kings. The Puranas call the dynasty the Andhras. A book of Prakrit verses, the Gathasaptashati, is credited to King Hala. |
| The Frieze | FRIEZE: carved panel pans scene by scene | The Satavahanas rose in the Deccan about the first century BCE, after the Mauryas. Simuka is named as the founder. Their capital was Pratishthana, now Paithan. Their greatest king was Gautamiputra Satakarni, about 106 to 130 CE. His mother, Gautami Balashri, left the Nashik inscription, which says he destroyed the Shakas and Pahlavas. Satavahana kings used their mother's name before their own, like Gautamiputra, son of Gautami. This shows that royal women were honoured. They gave land grants, free of tax, to Brahmins and Buddhist monks. This was the start of a long tradition. The Satavahanas were great patrons of art. The stupa gateways at Sanchi, the caves at Karle and the stupa at Amaravati belong to this age. Their coins were of lead, and their trade reached Rome through ports on the west coast. Prakrit was their language. |
| The Plaque | PLAQUE | Old name, new name. The Puranas call this dynasty the Andhras, after the region of Andhra. Inscriptions and coins call them the Satavahanas. Historians use the second name, since it is the one the rulers used themselves. |
| Inscription | INSCRIPTION: tip chiselled in | Carve this tip into memory. Satavahana: mother-names. tax-free grants, torana gates. Remember the mother-names and the free land grants. Both are favourite exam points. |

## Episode 25: The Sangam Age

**Inscription tip:** Chola tiger, Pandya fish, Chera bow. · **Sources lit:** texts, dug up, coins · **Timeline ribbon:** (-300, 300) · **Plaque:** Old name, new name: Tamilakam → Tamil Nadu and Kerala · **Exhibit:** Sangam palm leaf · **Label facts:** Tamil verses on a palm leaf; Sangam poems, about 300 BCE to 300 CE; Chola, Chera and Pandya kingdoms

| Segment | Visual | Narration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Dig | DIG: brush reveals the artefact | The brush clears a palm leaf. It is covered with Tamil verses, scratched with an iron stylus. These poems, collected much later, describe kings, wars, love and trade in the far south. What did a poet remember about the south? |
| How do we know? | SOURCES: evidence tags light up | How do we know? Sangam literature is the main source. Archaeologists dug up the port of Arikamedu and the town of Keezhadi. And Roman coins found in the south show the trade. |
| The Frieze | FRIEZE: carved panel pans scene by scene | Tamilakam was the land of the Tamils. Three kingdoms ruled it: the Cholas of Uraiyur, with the tiger, the Pandyas of Madurai, with the fish, and the Cheras of Vanji, with the bow. The Sangam literature gives us this world. The Tolkappiyam is a grammar. The Ettuthokai and Pattuppattu are collections of poems. The epics Silappadikaram and Manimekalai were composed a little later. Tradition says that poets met in assemblies, or Sangams, at Madurai. Modern scholars treat that as tradition, not as settled history. The poems divide the land into five landscapes, or tinais: hills, forest, farmland, seashore and desert. Each has its own moods and gods. Famous kings include Karikala Chola, who is credited with building embankments on the Kaveri, and Senguttuvan Chera. Trade was rich. Pepper, pearls and cloth went west. Roman gold coins came back in return. |
| The Plaque | PLAQUE | Old name, new name. The Sangam age is set in Tamilakam, the land of the Tamils. It covers most of present Tamil Nadu and Kerala. The old name is the name the poems themselves use. |
| Inscription | INSCRIPTION: tip chiselled in | Carve this tip into memory. Chola tiger, Pandya fish. Chera bow. Three kingdoms, three emblems. Add Uraiyur, Madurai and Vanji, and you have the south. |

## Episode 26: Gandhara and Mathura

**Inscription tip:** Gandhara: grey, Greek style. Mathura: red, Indian style. · **Sources lit:** dug up, inscriptions · **Timeline ribbon:** (0, 300) · **Plaque:** Scholars disagree: Gandhara first / Mathura first · **Exhibit:** Carved lotus · **Label facts:** A lotus, an early Buddhist symbol; Gandhara, Mathura, Amaravati; Three schools, three stones

| Segment | Visual | Narration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Dig | DIG: brush reveals the artefact | The brush clears a carved lotus, framed by a stone slab. In the early centuries, the Buddha was not shown as a figure. Artists used symbols. Later, two schools of sculpture took shape, Gandhara and Mathura. How can stone tell a story without a figure? |
| How do we know? | SOURCES: evidence tags light up | How do we know? Archaeologists dug up the monasteries of Taxila and Mathura. Donors' names are carved on the bases of statues. The sculptures themselves are the main evidence. |
| The Frieze | FRIEZE: carved panel pans scene by scene | Early Buddhist art used symbols. Footprints, a wheel, an empty throne and a tree stood for the Buddha. Around the first century CE, in the Kushana age, images began to appear. There are three main schools of sculpture of this time. The Gandhara school grew in the north-west. It drew on Greek and Roman ways of carving, such as wavy hair and folded robes. It used grey schist stone. The Mathura school grew near the Yamuna. It used local red sandstone, and its work was Indian in feel. Mathura made images for Buddhists, Jains and Hindus. The Amaravati school, in the Krishna valley, used white marble. It carved long, flowing stories on stupa railings. Each school left its own mark on the art that followed. |
| The Plaque | PLAQUE | Scholars disagree. Where did the first images of the Buddha come from? One view says they began in Gandhara, under Greek and Roman influence. Another view says they began in Mathura, from an Indian tradition of carving figures. Some scholars think that both centres worked at about the same time. |
| Inscription | INSCRIPTION: tip chiselled in | Carve this tip into memory. Gandhara: grey, Greek style. Mathura: red, Indian style. Grey and Greek, red and Indian. Add Amaravati's white marble, and you have three schools. |
| Dig Quiz | QUIZ: three artefact cards, then Mains | Dig Quiz. Pause and name it. Number one. Which school used grey schist stone? The answer: Gandhara. Number two. Which school used red sandstone? The answer: Mathura. Number three. Which school carved on white marble in the Krishna valley? The answer: Amaravati. Now one line for Mains. Compare the Gandhara and Mathura schools of art. |

## Episode 27: Trade With Rome

**Inscription tip:** Periplus, pepper, monsoon, Roman gold. · **Sources lit:** travellers' accounts, dug up, coins · **Timeline ribbon:** (-100, 300) · **Plaque:** Old name, new name: Barygaza → Bharuch · **Exhibit:** Roman-trade ship · **Label facts:** A ship that rode the monsoon; Trade with Rome, about 100 BCE to 300 CE; Pepper out, gold in

| Segment | Visual | Narration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Dig | DIG: brush reveals the artefact | The brush clears a wooden ship, with a square sail and a high stern. Ships like this carried pepper, pearls and cloth across the sea to Rome. They came back with gold. How did pepper pay for Roman gold? |
| How do we know? | SOURCES: evidence tags light up | How do we know? The Periplus of the Erythraean Sea, written by a Greek sailor about the first century CE, is a guide to ports. Archaeologists dug up Arikamedu. And Roman gold and silver coins are found in hoards in the south. |
| The Frieze | FRIEZE: carved panel pans scene by scene | Trade between India and the Roman world grew from about the first century BCE. It was by sea, helped by the monsoon winds. A Greek sailor named Hippalus is said to have discovered the pattern of the winds. The Periplus lists the ports. Muziris and Tondi were on the Malabar coast. Arikamedu was on the east coast. Barygaza, now Bharuch, was on the west coast. India sent spices, mainly pepper, along with pearls, ivory, cotton and fine cloth, and gems. Rome sent gold and silver coins, wine in jars called amphorae, glass and coral. Pliny, a Roman writer, complained that India drained Rome of gold, and gave a very large figure. Many traders from the Roman world settled on the coast. The trade also enriched Indian guilds, and the Satavahana and Tamil kingdoms. |
| The Plaque | PLAQUE | Old name, new name. The Periplus calls the western port Barygaza. Today the city is called Bharuch, in Gujarat. It stands on the Narmada, and has been a port for over two thousand years. |
| Inscription | INSCRIPTION: tip chiselled in | Carve this tip into memory. Periplus, pepper, monsoon. Roman gold. Four words to cover the Indo-Roman trade: a book, a spice, a wind and a coin. |
| Dig Quiz | QUIZ: three artefact cards, then Mains | Dig Quiz. Pause and name it. Number one. Which book is a Greek sailor’s guide to the ports? The answer: the Periplus. Number two. Which foreign coins reached South India? The answer: Roman gold and silver coins. Number three. Which Indian spice was the main export? The answer: pepper. Now one line for Mains. Examine the nature and impact of Indo-Roman trade. |
